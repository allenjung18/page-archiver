Tides of the Inner Basin

Twice a year, at the spring tides of March and September, the water in the inner basin rises over the quay wall and runs into Ropewalk Lane. Shopkeepers know the dates by heart; they stack sandbags the night before and open late the morning after.

The harbour board has measured the basin since 1887. Its gauge, a brass scale bolted to the lock gate, shows the highest tides creeping up by about two centimetres a decade. That is slow enough to ignore for a year and fast enough to matter for a lifetime.

This spring the board will vote on a plan to raise the quay wall by forty centimetres along its whole length. The engineers say it buys fifty years. The fishermen say it will make the steps too steep for anyone carrying a box of crabs, and they have asked for a ramp.

Nobody disputes the gauge. The argument is about who pays, and whether a wall is the right answer for a town that has always lived with the water rather than against it. The vote is on the second Tuesday of April, in the old customs house, and the public gallery is open.
